NovelForge vs Scrivener, Atticus, Obsidian and Microsoft Word

Scrivener, Atticus and Word are excellent, mature tools, and Obsidian is a superb notebook. This is not a claim that NovelForge beats them at everything. It is a plain account of where each one is strongest, so you can pick the right one, even when that is not us.

When NovelForge is the right choice

  • You want to own your files as ordinary Word documents, with no proprietary project format between you and your book.
  • You want no subscription, no account and no cloud, and nothing leaving your machine.
  • You write fiction with a world in it: many characters and places, invented calendars, a map to draw, and continuity to keep straight across a long manuscript.
  • You like a tool that is quick and keyboard-driven, and you use Windows.
  • You would like a tool whose source you can read and change.

When to pick something else

  • You are on a Mac, Linux or a phone. NovelForge is Windows-only for now. Scrivener (Mac and iOS) or Obsidian (everywhere) will serve you better today.
  • You need to typeset an ebook or a print interior. That is Atticus's specialty, and Scrivener's compile options go deep. NovelForge compiles a standard manuscript in Word; you would format it elsewhere.
  • You co-write live with another person. Word or Google Docs are built for that. NovelForge is a single-writer tool.
  • You already love Scrivener. If it works for you, keep using it. There is no import from Scrivener projects, though anything you can export to .docx can be brought in.

How each tool sees a book

Scrivener

The long-standing standard for long-form writers: a binder, corkboard, split editor, snapshots and a powerful compile system. It stores a book as a project bundle, so you work through Scrivener (or export) to get your words out. It has no continuity checker or map maker.

Atticus

A writing and formatting tool aimed at authors who publish their own books; its strength is turning a manuscript into polished ebook and print files. Its focus is formatting rather than planning structure or tracking a story's continuity.

Obsidian

A general-purpose notebook that stores plain Markdown and links notes into a graph. Many novelists use it for worldbuilding, and it has a huge plugin ecosystem. It is not fiction-specific, so scene structure, outlines and continuity checking are yours to assemble.

Microsoft Word

Where most manuscripts end up, and the format editors and agents expect. Word is excellent at a single document and awkward at a whole book of scenes. NovelForge leans on it deliberately: every scene is a Word file, so the two work together rather than competing.

NovelForge

Free, offline and made for fiction, with the planning, world and continuity tools a novelist otherwise pieces together from several apps, and a folder of ordinary Word files at the end of it.

Decide by trying it.

It is free and there is no account to create. You can walk away with your files at any time, because they are just Word documents.
